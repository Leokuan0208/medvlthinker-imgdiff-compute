▸Deep dive & terminology
What is a cascade? A cheap model answers first; a small gate decides whether to trust that answer or escalate the question to an expensive model. Most easy traffic is handled cheaply; only the uncertain questions pay for the big model.

Why compare to the 7B / 32B?

The 7 B and 32 B here are the numbers reported in the MedVLThinker paper for the exact checkpoints our project is built on. Comparing against them shows our contribution against the established baseline.

Why is the cascade ever above the 32B?

Because the cheap 7 B is right on a meaningful slice of questions the 32 B gets wrong (cross-model complementarity). When the gate keeps those on the 7 B, the cascade banks answers an always-32 B run would have lost — the "rescued − broken" surplus quantified in the escalation breakdown.

MedX-M

▸Deep dive & terminology

Why does the saving track the escalation rate?

The cheap 7 B pass is tiny — about 53 J against 6,883 J for a 32 B pass — so the cascade's cost is set almost entirely by what fraction of questions it escalates. SLAKE escalates least (52.9%) and saves most (47%); MedX-M escalates most (76%) and saves least (24%). MedXpert's hard questions are exactly the ones the gate (correctly) sends up, so the saving there is naturally smaller.

One honest caveat on the baseline

The always-32 B cost is read off the escalated (harder) subset of each dataset, which runs slightly longer than the easy questions. So on easy datasets the saving is a mild upper bound. The overall 0.639× energy figure already carries this same assumption, and the engine-independent FLOPs number (74% at cap320) does not depend on it at all.

Three views of the same saving. FLOPs (74% of always-32 B, cap320) · energy (0.639×, four-competent; 33.6% saved pooled) · wall-clock latency (33.6% saved pooled). They agree because the dominant cost in all three is "did the 32 B run on this question or not."

Wasted + redundant = the honest cost story

About 63% of escalations land in wasted (both wrong) or redundant (both right) — the 32 B ran but the answer didn't change. That is precisely why this is framed as an efficiency result with a modest accuracy bonus: the gate buys a small net gain by spending the big model mostly on questions it can't fix or didn't need to. the oracle analysis shows that no cheaper signal can tell those apart in advance.

Why run the floor in no-think mode
Reasoning tokens help only on the hard reasoning questions (PathVQA, MMMU, MedX-M) — and those are exactly the ones the margin gate escalates to the 32 B. So the cheap leg loses nothing by skipping its own reasoning: on the easy questions it keeps, no-think is actually more accurate (the +4 to +13 points on PMC, SLAKE, VQA-RAD), and on the hard ones the 32 B does the thinking instead. No-think at cap320 is the right floor, not a sacrifice.
▸Deep dive & terminology
No-think vs think. MedVLThinker is a reasoning model: in "think" mode it emits a chain of reasoning tokens before answering. "No-think" forces a direct answer. The 7 B leg runs no-think so it is cheap (one short generation); the 32 B leg runs think (up to 2048 tokens) only when escalated.

Why can no-think beat think on perception?

On recognition-style questions (what organ, normal vs abnormal, which modality) a long reasoning chain mostly adds room to talk itself out of a correct first impression. Removing it removes that failure mode, so the direct answer is often better — visible here as +9.2 on SLAKE and +13.2 on VQA-RAD. On genuine multi-step reasoning (MMMU, MedXpert) the chain earns its keep, so no-think trails there.

Is this apples-to-apples?

The baseline 7 B numbers are the reported MedVLThinker-7B figures; our column is the same 7 B weights run no-think at cap320. The gap therefore reflects two deliberate cheapening choices (no reasoning, lower resolution) — and the floor still comes out ahead on the general sets, which is what makes a cheap leg viable.

What this buys the cascade

A stronger floor means fewer questions need escalation to clear a given accuracy bar — part of why the gate holds parity while escalating only ~63% of traffic.

Methods · Router design

How the cascade routes a question

One frozen rule, computed in milliseconds. The cheap 7 B answers every question; a single number — its confidence margin — decides whether to trust it or escalate to the 32 B.

  • 7 B leg (the floor). Runs on every question in no-think mode at reduced resolution (cap320), so it is cheap — about 53 J and a fifth of a second per query.
  • The gate. Reads the 7 B's answer-option log-probabilities, computes the margin m = logp(top1) − logp(top2), and escalates whenever m < τ.
  • 32 B leg (only on escalation). Runs in think mode (up to 2048 reasoning tokens); its answer becomes final. On kept questions the 32 B card stays idle — which is exactly why the measured energy reflects the real saving.
Why this design at all
The whole method rests on cross-model complementarity: the cheap 7 B is right on about a third of the questions the 32 B gets wrong. A perfect gate would beat always-32 B by +12.1 pp. The margin gate captures the easy part of that surplus cheaply; the oracle analysis measures how much of the rest is reachable.
Methods · The signal

What “margin” means

The margin is the model's confidence gap: how far its top answer option sits above its second choice, in log-probability. A big gap means the 7 B is committed; a small gap means it is torn — and torn is exactly when the 32 B is worth paying for.

Top option towers over the rest → m is large → m ≥ τ → the cheap answer is trusted.

Top two options are almost level → m is small → m < τ → the question is sent to the 32 B.

Why this signal, and not something fancier
The margin is computed from the first answer token the 7 B produces — no extra forward pass, no second model. We tested richer alternatives (full option distribution, entropy, hidden-state probes); none separated “will the 32 B rescue this?” meaningfully better. The parameter-free margin is both the cheapest signal and, empirically, as good as anything we tried (see the router comparison).
▸Deep dive & terminology
Log-probability. For each answer choice (A, B, C…) the model assigns a probability; its logarithm is the log-prob. Higher (closer to 0) = more likely. The margin subtracts the second-highest from the highest, so it measures separation, not absolute confidence.

A concrete number

If the top option scores −0.05 and the next scores −3.55, the margin is 3.50 — a wide, confident gap. If they score −0.70 and −0.90, the margin is just 0.20 — a coin-flip the gate will escalate.

One subtlety we were careful about

The gate is evaluated against whether the 7 B's actually decoded answer is correct — not against the option ranking the margin is read from. Scoring it the lazy way (margin vs. its own argmax) would inflate the signal by construction; using the independently generated answer keeps the measurement honest.

Methods · Calibrating the gate

How the threshold τ = 0.426 was set

The gate has exactly one knob, and it is fixed before touching the evaluation. We learn the margin→reliability mapping on a held-out training split, pick the cut that hits our error budget, freeze it, and apply the same number everywhere.

The recipe

  • Collect. Run the 7 B over the PMC-VQA training split; for each question record its margin and whether its decoded answer was right.
  • Fit. A StandardScaler → LogisticRegression on the single margin feature gives P(7B correct | m).
  • Cut. Choose τ as the margin quantile that meets the target error-rate budget — here τ = 0.426.
  • Freeze. The same τ is applied unchanged to all six evaluation benchmarks. No per-dataset tuning.

Schematic: reliability rises with margin; τ is the cut that meets the error budget.

✓ Two properties that make this safe to publish
Contamination-clean: the m23k RL training is text-only, so the model never saw PMC-VQA images during RL, and the PMC train split used to fit τ is disjoint from the 2,000 PMC items in the evaluation. Transfers across resolution: a τ fit at one image resolution still calibrates the gate when the 7 B is served at another (shown in the resolution sweep) — the margin scale is stable enough that one number generalises.
▸Deep dive & terminology
Error-rate budget. Rather than picking τ to maximise a score on the eval (which would overfit), we fix the tolerable rate of “kept-but-wrong” 7 B answers in advance and solve for the τ that delivers it on the training split. The eval then reports what that pre-committed choice actually achieves.

Why a held-out split and not the eval itself?

Fitting the threshold on the same data you report on is the classic way to manufacture a result that won't replicate. Fitting on PMC-VQA train and freezing means every evaluation number is genuinely out-of-sample for the gate.

Why logistic regression on one feature?

It is the simplest calibrated map from a real-valued score to a probability. A heavier model (tested in the router comparison) buys nothing here, so the one-parameter version is preferred for transparency and stability.

Methods · Did anything beat the simple gate?

Other routers we tried

We froze the margin gate — and froze a learned gradient-boosted (HistGBM) router and a conformal (CP-Router-style) router, each calibrated on PMC-train — then applied all three unchanged to every one of the six datasets (n=8,220). Averaged the way the base paper reports — macro mean over datasets — the parameter-free 1-D margin gate is the best routing rule of the three; the richer routers do not transfer.

Also tried, also did not help
Hidden-state probe (layer-14 activations), predictive entropy, and the full answer-option distribution were each tested as escalation signals — none separated rescuable questions better than the margin. Separately, single-model 4-arm routing (think×RAG inside one model) is structurally dead: four views of the same model share its blind spots, so its oracle sits far below the luck floor. The win requires two different models, which is why the cascade is cross-model.
▸Deep dive & terminology
Frozen transfer. Each router is calibrated once on PMC-train (the margin threshold, the HistGBM classifier, the conformal quantile), then frozen and applied unchanged to all six datasets — the realistic deployment test. A router that needs per-dataset retuning to compete is not deployable; the margin gate needs none.
Conformal / CP-Router. A router that uses a held-out calibration set to attach formal confidence sets to predictions and escalates when the set is ambiguous (Su et al., arXiv 2505.19970). It is the natural sophisticated competitor — and on the matched vLLM ablation it never beats the margin gate: it ties at 63.0 on the six-benchmark macro and trails 69.1 vs 69.2 excl-MedX-M. (The table's Margin row shows the deployed HF accuracy, 64.06.)

Why this is the right thing to report

The honest headline is not “our router is best” — it is “the cheapest possible router is already on the efficient frontier, so complexity is unjustified.” For a deployable system that is the stronger claim: ship the 1-D margin gate.

Methods · The ceiling, and why we can't reach it

Oracle headroom & a falsification test

A perfect gate would do far better than ours. The honest question is whether any cheap signal could close that gap — so we pre-registered a test for it. It says: the signal is real, but most of the headroom is genuinely out of reach.

The falsification test

For each escalated question, can a signal predict in advance whether the 32 B will rescue it? We pre-registered an AUROC bar of 0.60 — below that, the signal is noise.

What it means

  • The signal clears the 0.60 bar — rescuability is weakly predictable, so the accuracy the routing adds is real, not luck.
  • But the oracle behaves like AUROC ≈ 0.788 at 16% escalation — near-perfect targeting. No cheap signal comes close.
  • So the +11.1 pp headroom is mostly unreachable. The deployable result is an efficiency win with a modest, honestly-bounded accuracy bonus.
Why report the ceiling we miss
Showing the oracle and then proving we can't reach it is the opposite of overclaiming. It tells a reviewer exactly how much is left on the table (a lot) and that we looked hard for it (margin, entropy, full distribution, hidden states, a learned union) before concluding the cheap gate is the right deployable answer.
Methods · How cheap can the 7B leg get?

Image resolution vs. cost — and why cap320

The 7 B's cost scales with how many visual tokens its image is worth. We swept the cap from full-res down to 80 tokens. Cascade accuracy holds at parity with always-32 B all the way down, so the only question is how much compute to bank.

Why cap320 and not the cheapest point
Cascade accuracy 0.572 exactly equals always-32 B at cap320, while banking a free ~5 points of compute over full-res. Pushing to cap80 would save a little more but starts to dent the 7 B leg (0.514) and slips cascade accuracy to 0.567 — below exact parity. cap320 is the principled choice: maximum saving that still holds parity exactly. (The grid's cheapest-while-tied point, train cap80 / infer cap160 at 68%, needs a different gate artifact; we kept the single frozen gate for a cleaner deployable story.)
▸Deep dive: calibration transfer
The transfer property. A threshold fit at one resolution still calibrates the gate when the 7 B is served at another. The fitted τ barely moves across caps — full-res 0.426, cap640 0.424, cap320 0.428, cap160 0.415, cap80 0.404 — so one frozen number works across the whole range.

Why the margin scale is stable

Shrinking the image mostly adds noise to hard questions (where the 7 B was already unsure and would escalate anyway) and leaves easy ones (wide margins) almost untouched. So the decision boundary — which lives between those two regimes — stays put even as resolution drops.

What “backbone %” counts

It is prefill-inclusive: it charges for processing the prompt and image, not just for generated tokens. A decode-only proxy looked far rosier (~59%); we report the honest ~74% at cap320 instead.
