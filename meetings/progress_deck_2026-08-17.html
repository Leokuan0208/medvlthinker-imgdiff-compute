The gap that drives the whole project: a correct answer is present in the pool 63% of the time, and we end up giving one 49% of the time. Closing that gap is what a verifier is for.
6 · What we built

What the verifier actually is

  • A LoRA adapter on the same 7B — 47.6 million parameters, 182 MB on disk
  • Trained on questions and images the evaluation never sees
  • Scores P(correct | image, question, candidate) and we keep the best
A LoRA adapter is not a model. It cannot see an image or read text on its own. It is a set of correction matrices applied on top of the 7B — so scoring one candidate costs a full pass through all 8.29 billion parameters.

10 · Why the verifier costs so much

The image encoder re-runs 294 tokens to recover five

  • Language prefill is shared across the eight candidates — 1.16×
  • The image encoder is not — 4.74× for eight answers
  • The image occupies positions 31–325; the cache is block-aligned to 320
Five tokens past a block boundary, so the engine cannot mark the image as already computed — and re-encodes all 294 of its tokens to recover the last five.
11 · The pivot

We were asking the wrong question

Can a small model plus extra computation match the big one?
How much can we improve the small model, for free?
  • On free text the big model is about ten times more efficient per point of accuracy
  • Sampling eight answers and scoring them costs more than asking the big model once — and is less accurate

  • The learned version scored worse — −0.0109 [−0.0245, +0.0034] — and broke the no-cell-goes-backwards guardrail
One consequence to be aware of. A rank only has meaning within its own question, so the fused number cannot double as a confidence signal. Fed to the escalation gate it collapsed to 15 distinct values across 2,345 questions and the controller degenerated — two cells escalating everything, one escalating nothing.
And yet we are going to recommend the head alone. The fusion is the better selector and it is the safe one. But it can only be bought by running the adapter, which means a full pass through the 7B for every candidate — and that is the cost the rest of this report is about removing.

Dropping the LoRA costs 0.006 of accuracy and removes every scoring pass. On its own the head is the better of the two selectors — it converts 0.8025 of the available gain against the adapter's 0.7752 — and it is the only one whose cost can be driven to zero, because it reads a number the generator has already computed. Capturing it instead of recomputing it changes 15 of 2,345 picks.
Three things we are not hiding. The head alone is not guardrail-clean — VQA-RAD-open drops 0.015 (n=200, not significant). Its advantage over the adapter is judge-specific: under strict string matching the ordering reverses (−0.0145). And the free capture above runs at full resolution; captured at the resolution the generator actually uses it reads 0.4947, because the head was trained on full-resolution features. Refitting it is a fifteen-second job and has not been done.

Two forces pulling opposite ways. Hotter sampling puts a correct answer in the pool more often (column 3 rises) and makes it harder to identify (column 4 falls). Accuracy is exactly their product, so the best setting sits in the middle — at 0.4, not the 0.7 we had been using. It only helps because something is choosing. Sampling at 0.0 and keeping the single answer is a significant loss against sampling at 0.7 and selecting. Diversity is worth nothing on its own; it is worth something only to a selector that can exploit it.

This is measured with the LoRA verifier doing the picking, on the same 2,345-question pool. The head has never been evaluated at 0.4 — it was trained on candidates drawn at 0.7 — which is why retraining it at 0.4 is our first next step.

21 · The prompt bias

The fix is one sentence, and it is a deletion

We separated “which options” from “in which order”. Keeping the sentence and only swapping the order — “output 'no' or 'yes'” — raised the bias on all three cells (+0.069→+0.078 on PathVQA) and cost −0.0089 of accuracy there, a significant loss. It is the answer space being supplied at all, not the order it is supplied in.

24 · How we keep ourselves honest

We made the measurement stricter three times

  • Matched the prompts. Our two arms differed in the answer format they requested, as well as in the thing we were testing. Correcting it strengthened one finding and dissolved another.
  • Dropped a contaminated benchmark. The 7B scored 0.80 against its own published 54.0. Removing the image dropped it to 0.59; a control model scored 0.57.
  • Switched to equal weighting per cell.
Each one made our headline smaller — and we found all three ourselves, before anyone else read the work.
25 · How we keep ourselves honest

What did not work

  • Making the candidate pool more diverse. More variety, no better selection.
  • Scoring the multiple-choice options with the verifier instead of trusting the model's own pick. Beaten by the model's own answer on all four option cells — two of them significant losses.
  • Deciding adaptively how many answers to sample. A fixed number, chosen once, beats it.
  • Voting among the sampled answers. Helps on two cells, does nothing on five, and hurts if you sample too few.
26 · Closing

Where we stand

  • The verifier is no longer the expensive part. A 918-thousand-parameter head reading a vector the generator already produced picks better than a 47-million-parameter adapter that costs a full model pass. Free text goes 0.4495 → 0.5023 at 2.37× the 7B's compute today, 1.20× once the image encoder is fixed — and past the 32B reasoning baseline either way.
  • A free, significant improvement that survives the hardest test we have. Deleting one sentence of the benchmark's own prompt is worth +0.0052 on the eight-cell average at 1.000× compute, and it grows when the answer key is balanced.
  • An honest map of the limits. Selection converts 0.78–0.81 of the available gain no matter what we build — about 27 architectures now — and that is a property of the problem, not of our engineering.
  • What is still open. The prompt effect lives on one cell. The head has never been evaluated at the temperature we now want to sample at. And nothing has been run end to end.
The shape of the claim has changed. It used to be “a 7B plus compute can match a 32B”, which was true and cost more than the 32B. It is now “a small trained verifier improves a 7B on its three free-text cells — two of them significantly — at 1.2× its own compute, and a prompt correction improves it on a fourth cell for free” — a smaller claim, and one that survives its own cost accounting.
27 · Closing

What we do next

1
Retrain the head at the temperature we actually want to sample at.

The head was trained on candidates drawn at 0.7 and the ladder says we should be drawing at 0.4. Nobody has measured the head on a 0.4 pool — it needs one fresh capture per generation seed, then about fifteen seconds of CPU per seed to refit. It should also be refitted on the resolution the generator actually runs at, which is the one place the free capture currently loses accuracy.

2
Check the other benchmarks for the same instruction defect.

Two of three yes/no cells carry the sentence. If the same pattern shows up in the multiple-choice harnesses and in other model families, this stops being one result on one dataset and becomes a general finding about how these systems are evaluated.

3
Merge the pieces into one method and run it end to end.

Prompt fix on the yes/no cells · head selection on free text · sampling at 0.4 · states captured during generation · the image encoded once. Right now each is measured on its own, by re-scoring saved outputs. Nothing has ever been executed as a single program — and until it is, the combined number is an expectation, not a measurement.

Why we expect them to compose. They act on different cells and at different stages: the prompt fix touches the two yes/no cells, the head touches the three free-text cells, the temperature and the encoding fix touch cost rather than accuracy. There is no cell where two of them contend for the same gain. That is a reason to expect it, not a substitute for measuring it.
